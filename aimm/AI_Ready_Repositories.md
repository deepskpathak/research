# AI-Ready Repositories

## Assessing AI maturity, effectiveness and readiness from the codebase up, with deep case studies of Google and JPMorgan Chase and a roadmap for regulated financial institutions

**Working paper, version 2.0. September 2026.**
Written from the standpoint of an AI/ML researcher and Distinguished Engineer in a large technology organization operating under regulatory and compliance obligations. All sources are public.

---

### Abstract

Roughly nine in ten organizations report regular AI use, yet the share attributing any enterprise-level earnings impact to AI has been flat at 37-39% for two years and only about 6% qualify as high performers [1, 2]. In software engineering the gap is sharper still: coding agents are the most-scaled class of enterprise agent, one in three pull requests on GitHub now involves an agent [3], and the controlled evidence on their effect ranges from a 26% increase in completed tasks to a 19% slowdown depending on the codebase and task [4, 5]. This paper argues that the missing unit of assessment is the code repository. Enterprise maturity models score strategy, data and governance; developer productivity metrics score people; model risk management scores models. None scores whether a repository is in a state that lets an agent act on it reliably, safely and measurably.

The paper does four things. First, it states the problem: an adoption-value gap, a measurement gap, a model gap and a governance gap, each of which resolves to the repository. Second, it answers what a repository needs to be called AI-ready and mature, drawing on environment-setup benchmarks, flaky-test research, code-health economics, instruction-file experiments and vendor guidance, and proposes the Repository AI-Readiness Model (RARM): eight dimensions with computable indicators, scored on the minimum across dimensions, with an estate-level maturity ladder above it. Third, it surveys what the industry and academia have and presents two deep case studies from public sources: Google, whose monorepo, universal code review, static analysis, continuous testing and large-scale-change machinery made it ready for AI before the models arrived; and JPMorgan Chase, whose AI program shows how a regulated bank scales from assistants to agents while measuring outcomes. Fourth, it sets out what a regulated, fintech or compliance-bound company can do: a mapping from supervisory rules on secure development, change management and segregation of duties (MAS TRM, DORA, FFIEC, NYDFS, PCI DSS) to repository controls for agent-authored code, an evidence pack for auditors, and a 12-month roadmap.

**Keywords:** AI maturity model, AI-ready codebase, agentic software engineering, repository readiness, developer productivity measurement, model risk management, secure development lifecycle.

## 1. Introduction, scope and definitions

### 1.1 Why the repository

Every enterprise AI maturity model published between 2018 and 2026 has a "data" dimension, and most have "technology," "governance" and "people." None has a dimension for the artifact that coding agents actually touch: the repository, with its build, tests, documentation, ownership rules and pull-request workflow. That omission was tolerable when AI meant dashboards and models; it is not tolerable when McKinsey finds roughly 20% of organizations, and 31% of large enterprises, scaling software-coding agents [1], Google reports that 75% of its new code is AI-generated and approved by engineers [6], and Gartner projects that 90% of enterprise software engineers will use AI code assistants by 2028, up from under 14% in early 2024 [7].

The evidence assembled in Section 3 shows why the repository is the right unit. The same model, harness and task produce different outcomes depending on whether the repository can be set up and run, whether its tests mean anything, whether its structure can be navigated, and whether its workflow can verify and govern a change. Those are properties of the codebase and the organization around it, not of the model. DORA's finding that AI "amplifies what's already there" is the same observation at the level of the team [8].

### 1.2 Scope

The paper covers three levels and connects them: the organization (strategy, governance, platform, people, measurement), the engineering estate (the portfolio of repositories and the process that manages their readiness), and the repository (a single codebase and its workflow). "Repository" means a code repository. Documentation stores, wikis and data assets appear only as knowledge sources that a repository or workflow reaches. Case studies use public sources only. Financial services receives the most weight in Section 5 because it is where the regulatory lens on the software development lifecycle is sharpest.

### 1.3 Definitions

- **AI readiness** is a pre-adoption, purpose-specific property: whether an organization's assets, capabilities and commitment are adequate for a particular AI adoption purpose [9]. Readiness is task-relative, as data can be ready for one question and unready for another [10].
- **AI maturity** is a post-adoption, staged property: the degree to which practices are defined, repeatable, measured and continuously improved, in the lineage of the Capability Maturity Model [11].
- **AI effectiveness** is the measured system-level outcome: change in delivery performance, quality, cost and risk attributable to AI, not inferred from individual usage or self-report.
- A **coding agent** is a system in which a language model directs its own process and tool use to change a codebase, as distinct from a completion assistant [12]. A **harness** is the runtime that wraps a model with tools, context management, permissions and an execution environment. A **context file** is a repository-level instruction file (AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, copilot-instructions.md, Cursor rules) read by a harness [13-15].
- An **AI-ready repository** is one whose environment, verification signals, structure, documentation, workflow and telemetry allow a coding agent to complete a defined class of tasks with measured reliability under the organization's controls. A **mature estate** is one in which readiness is inventoried, scored, remediated and re-measured as a repeatable process.

### 1.4 Contributions

1. A statement of the assessment problem that resolves each of its four components to the repository (Section 2).
2. The Repository AI-Readiness Model (RARM): eight evidence-backed dimensions, computable indicators, a minimum-across scoring rule, task-class tiers, and a five-level estate maturity ladder (Section 3).
3. A landscape survey and two deep case studies, Google and JPMorgan Chase, each mapped onto the model's dimensions, with Goldman Sachs, DBS, Citi and Morgan Stanley as comparators (Section 4).
4. A regulated-company playbook: a control mapping from supervisory SDLC rules to agent-authored code, an audit evidence pack, an effectiveness metric set and a 12-month roadmap (Section 5).

## 2. The problem

### 2.1 Adoption has outrun value, and the difference is organizational

Independent surveys from 2025 and 2026 agree on the shape of the problem. McKinsey's 2026 survey (n=1,719) finds nearly nine in ten organizations using AI in at least one function and 44% scaled enterprise-wide, while only 37% attribute any EBIT impact to AI (39% the prior year) and 6% qualify as high performers [1, 2]. BCG finds 5% of companies "future-built" and 60% reaping "hardly any material value" [16]. IBM's CEO study reports that 25% of AI initiatives delivered expected ROI and 16% scaled enterprise-wide [17]. Deloitte finds 21% with mature agent governance [18]; Capgemini finds 2% with fully scaled agents and trust in fully autonomous agents falling from 43% to 27% in a year [19]. Gartner projects that through 2026 organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects unsupported by AI-ready data [20], and that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027 for "escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls" [21]. The most-cited failure statistic, MIT NANDA's "95% get zero return," rests on 52 interviews and 153 survey responses and is not derived in the report; it belongs in the record only with that caveat [22, 23].

What separates the few from the many is organizational rather than technical. McKinsey's high performers are almost three times as likely to have fundamentally redesigned workflows (73% versus 25%) [1]. Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index attributes 67% of AI impact to organizational factors and 32% to individual ones [24]. BCG's 10-20-70 rule puts 70% of the effort on people and processes [16, 25]. MIT CISR's four-stage model, built on 721 companies, finds the largest financial step between "build pilots and capabilities" and "develop AI ways of working" [26, 27]. For software engineering, "ways of working" are encoded in repositories and their workflows, which is where this paper looks.

### 2.2 The measurement gap: what is counted is not what matters

**Perception is not measurement.** METR's randomized trial gave 16 experienced open-source developers 246 real tasks on repositories they knew; they forecast a 24% speedup, believed afterwards they had been 20% faster, and were measured 19% slower [5]. METR's 2026 follow-up found the slowdown no longer distinguishable from zero but the design compromised by self-selection and task substitution [28], and its survey work warns that self-reported time savings have overestimated measured savings by about 40 percentage points [29].

**Task-level gains are real and bounded.** Three field experiments with 4,867 developers found a 26.08% increase in completed tasks (standard error 10.3%) [4]; Google's enterprise RCT with 96 engineers found roughly 21% less time on task with a wide confidence interval [30]; the original Copilot experiment found 55.8% on one greenfield task [31]. All measure bounded, well-specified work; METR shows formally that an arbitrarily high speedup on observed tasks is consistent with an arbitrarily small uplift in value [32].

**System-level delivery does not follow automatically.** DORA's 2024 report associated a 25% increase in AI adoption with 1.5% lower throughput and 7.2% lower stability [33]; its 2025 report found throughput now positive but stability still negative [8]. Faros AI's telemetry across roughly 10,000 developers found high-adoption teams merging 98% more pull requests with review time up 91% and no organization-level throughput gain; its 2026 follow-up across 22,000 developers reported incidents per PR tripling and deployments per week falling 11.7% [34]. GitClear's 2026 analysis of 623 million code changes found duplication up 81% and refactoring down to 3.8% of changed lines [35]; Uplevel found 41% more bugs with no cycle-time change [36]; Veracode found roughly 45% of AI-generated samples failing OWASP checks in both 2025 and spring 2026, unchanged across model generations when no security guidance was supplied [37].

**Exposure metrics are not comparable.** Google reports 75% of new code AI-generated and approved [6]; Microsoft 20-30% [38]; Coinbase about 40% [39]; Anthropic's spokesperson 70-90% [40]. The denominators (characters, lines, commits), attribution windows and quality qualifiers differ. Google's own management metric is different in kind: a "carefully measured" increase in engineering velocity of about 10%, defined as capacity created in hours per week [41]. Trust lags usage throughout: 84% of developers use or plan to use AI tools, 33% trust the output, and 66% are frustrated by solutions that are "almost right, but not quite" [42].

### 2.3 The model gap: no instrument scores the codebase

The maturity-model literature has known its weaknesses for fifteen years. Becker et al. derived eight design requirements, including comparison with existing models and iterative evaluation [43]; Pöppelbuß and Röglinger distinguished descriptive models from prescriptive ones that supply improvement measures [44]; Sadiq et al.'s systematic review found 87% of AI maturity models descriptive and only 47% empirically validated [45]. Industry models follow the pattern: Gartner scores seven pillars on five levels whose names changed between editions [46]; Microsoft's 2026 agentic model has five CMM-style levels and five pillars [47]; Salesforce's is an autonomy ladder [48]; only MIT CISR, BCG and Accenture connect stage to financial outcome, all by self-reported staging [16, 26, 49].

The closest existing instruments to the codebase are DORA's 2025 AI Capabilities Model, whose seven capabilities include "AI-accessible internal data," "healthy data ecosystems," "strong version control practices" and "working in small batches" [8], and the ML production-readiness rubrics of the previous decade: the ML Test Score's 28 tests scored on the minimum across sections [50] and Amershi et al.'s six-attribute Activity Maturity Index [51]. DORA's capabilities are team-level survey constructs; the ML rubrics were written for models, not repositories. Neither tells an engineering leader which of 3,000 repositories an agent can be trusted with.

### 2.4 The governance gap: agents act on code that regulators care about

Three developments make the gap urgent. First, capability horizons are lengthening: METR's 50%-reliability time horizon has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 and every three months since 2024 [52], and JPMorgan's chief analytics officer described 2026 as "the era of long-running autonomous agents" operating for "an hour or two," with a path to "multiple hours, then days, then weeks" [53]. Second, the security and supply-chain surface has grown: tool-poisoning attacks hide instructions in tool descriptions [54]; OWASP's Top 10 for Agentic Applications names memory and context poisoning, tool misuse and agentic supply-chain risks [55]; FINOS's AI Governance Framework catalogues agent-specific risks including MCP server supply-chain compromise and agent-mediated credential discovery [56]. Third, the regulatory evidence set is shifting. US banking agencies replaced SR 11-7 with SR 26-2 in April 2026 and placed generative and agentic AI explicitly outside model risk management pending an interagency request for information [57, 58]. Singapore's MAS has proposed AI risk management guidelines that name AI agents and require inventories, materiality tiering, kill switches and version control of code, data and parameters [59]. The EU's Digital Omnibus delayed high-risk AI Act obligations to December 2027 but left the evidence set intact [60], while DORA's technical standards already require static and dynamic source-code testing and independent change approval for any ICT change [61]. Supervisors quantify the gap themselves: 75% of UK financial firms use AI with a third of use cases involving third parties [62]; more than 85% of large euro-area banks use AI [63]; ASIC found governance "lagging" adoption [64]; FINMA found most institutions "still in the early stages" of AI governance [65].

### 2.5 Problem statement

> An engineering organization needs an instrument that (a) scores, per repository and per task class, whether a coding agent can be expected to succeed reliably and safely; (b) aggregates those scores into an estate-level maturity that can be managed as a repeatable process; (c) measures effectiveness at the system level with outcome metrics rather than usage or self-report; (d) produces evidence in the shape regulators and auditors expect for changes to production code; and (e) satisfies the design requirements of a defensible maturity model.

No published model does all five. Section 3 builds the repository layer from evidence; Section 4 shows what the industry has and how two large organizations have solved parts of the problem; Section 5 adds the regulated-company layer.

## 3. What a repository needs to be called AI-ready and mature

### 3.1 The evidence: what determines whether an agent succeeds on a codebase

The benchmark and field literature of 2023-2026 allows a reasonably confident ranking of the properties that condition agent success. We group the evidence by property, strongest first.

**Executability.** An agent that cannot build and run the code cannot verify anything it writes. Automated environment setup is the least solved problem in the agent stack: on EnvBench, the best approach configured 6.69% of 329 Python repositories and 29.47% of 665 JVM repositories with "genuine configuration challenges" [66]; Installamatic installed 55% of 40 Python projects at least once in ten attempts [67]; ExecutionAgent ran the test suites of 33 of 50 projects across 14 languages [68]; Repo2Run reached 86% on 420 Python repositories that already had unit tests, and its authors describe environment building as "the primary bottleneck" for executable code datasets [69]. Infrastructure matters even when setup succeeds: identical model, harness and tasks produced a 6-point gap on Terminal-Bench across VM resource configurations [70]. GitHub's coding agent pre-installs dependencies through a setup workflow so the agent can "hit the ground running" instead of discovering them by trial and error [71]; OpenAI's Codex runs each task in a sandbox preloaded with the repository, with internet off during the agent phase [72]. Hermetic builds and dev containers are the organizational prerequisites [73].

**Verification signal.** Tests are the agent's only ground truth, and their quality bounds what any measurement of agent success can mean. SWE-bench Verified doubled the measured success of the same model (16% to 33.2%) by removing underspecified issues, overly specific tests and broken environments [74]; SWE-Bench+ found 31.08% of "passing" patches passed only because of weak tests and 32.67% because the solution leaked in the issue text or comments [75]. Flakiness is the everyday form of the problem: at Google, about 1.5% of test runs report a flaky result, almost 16% of tests show some flakiness, and about 84% of observed pass-to-fail transitions involve a flaky test [76]; root causes were catalogued a decade ago [77]. Static analysis is verification that scales: Google's Tricorder runs more than 100 analyzers with an effective false-positive rate just under 5%, and authors apply automated fixes about 3,000 times a day because the findings are surfaced in code review [78, 79]. Feeding static-analysis warnings back to the model fixed up to 55.5% of security weaknesses in AI-generated snippets [80], which matters because roughly 40% of AI-generated programs in security-relevant scenarios were vulnerable in the earliest study and the rate has barely moved since [37, 81]. Production migrations show the same dependence on verification loops: Airbnb migrated 3,500 test files in six weeks with a validation state machine that fed errors back to the model, reaching 75% in a four-hour run and 97% after four days of tuning [82], and Amazon's Java-upgrade agent, credited with an estimated 4,500 developer-years saved, operates on a task class where compilation and existing tests define done [83]. GitHub scopes its coding agent to "well-tested codebases" [84]; Anthropic's guidance reduces to "if you can't verify it, don't ship it" [14].

**Structure and navigability.** Repository-level tasks require "understanding and coordinating changes across multiple functions, classes, and even files" [85]; cross-file context is the decisive input in CrossCodeEval and RepoBench [86, 87]; a line-level repository graph improved every agent it was plugged into [88]; retrieval helps but retrievers, not generators, are the bottleneck [89]. Code health has a measured business cost independent of AI: across 39 production codebases, low-quality code contained 15 times more defects and took 124% more development time to change [90]. Thoughtworks' "AI-friendly code design" blip makes the connection explicit: expressive naming "provides domain context," modularity and abstractions "keep AI's context manageable by limiting necessary changes," and "good software design for humans also benefits AI" [91]. Google's monorepo case shows the counterintuitive corollary: a single repository of billions of lines is navigable because of its tooling (Piper, code search, OWNERS files, a build graph) rather than its size [92, 93].

**Documentation and decisions.** Retrieving documentation improved code generation by 52% relative on CoNaLa [94], and library documentation is the most useful retrieval source in CodeRAG-Bench [89]. But most READMEs "lack information regarding the purpose and status" of a project [95], and practitioners report documentation issues dominated by staleness and incompleteness. Architecture decision records give agents the "why" that code cannot express: "a collection of records for 'architecturally significant' decisions" kept in version control [96].

**Context files.** The only controlled study of repository instruction files found that they "do not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20% on average"; developer-written files beat LLM-generated ones (p=0.038); explicit command and tool instructions were followed reliably; and "repository overviews, although popular and recommended by model providers, are not helpful" [97]. Vendor guidance agrees: keep CLAUDE.md concise and ask of each line "would removing this cause Claude to make mistakes?" [14]; keep rules under 500 lines and add one only when the agent repeats a mistake [15]; treat AGENTS.md as "a README for agents" with setup commands, test instructions and PR conventions [13, 98]. Procedures that are only sometimes relevant belong in on-demand skills or playbooks rather than the always-loaded file [99, 100]. Context is a finite resource whose value declines with length [101-103].

**Task specification.** On the hardest public benchmark, "wrong solution" is the largest failure category (35-50%) [104]. GitHub's ideal task "includes: a clear description of the problem to be solved or the work required," acceptance criteria and the files to change [71]. Specification discipline is a repository property when it lives in issue templates and acceptance tests; Amazon's Kiro makes it a first-class artifact with requirements, design and tasks carrying acceptance criteria and hooks that trigger tests on events [105], and Anthropic's harness research finds generator/evaluator separation and pre-negotiated "sprint contracts" to be strong levers on long-running work [106].

**Ownership, workflow and guardrails.** Ownership has predicted defects for fifteen years: the number of low-expertise contributors and the share of the top owner relate to both pre-release faults and post-release failures [107]. Google's OWNERS files are the mechanism by which its large-scale-change tooling shards and routes hundreds of automated changes a day for review [78]. In modern forges the equivalents are CODEOWNERS with required owner review [108], rulesets that enforce required reviews, status checks and signed commits and resolve conflicts to "the most restrictive version of the rule" [109], and harness-side hooks that "execute automatically at specific points" to block destructive commands, run linters and audit tool use [110]. GitHub's coding agent can push only to its own branches and requires an approver different from the assigner [84]. Sonar's quality gate for agentic AI adds a dependency-risk condition because "AI agents have autonomy to write code and introduce dependencies" [111].

**Measurement.** Without a private task suite and telemetry, none of the above can be shown to work. Public benchmarks are contaminated and infrastructure-sensitive [70, 75]; enterprises need 20-50 real tasks per task class with pass^k (all of k trials succeed) rather than pass@k, because reliability determines what can be delegated [112, 113].

### 3.2 The Repository AI-Readiness Model (RARM)

RARM turns the evidence into an instrument with three properties borrowed from the ML production-readiness lineage: computable indicators rather than interview answers, a minimum-across scoring rule [50], and task-relative readiness [10]. It has eight dimensions, each scored 0-4. Table 3.1 gives the anchor descriptions for scores 0, 2 and 4 and the indicators that can be computed from version control, CI, code-analysis and harness telemetry.

**Table 3.1. RARM dimensions, anchors and indicators**

| Dimension | Score 0 | Score 2 | Score 4 | Computable indicators |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **R1 Executability and environment determinism** | Build depends on a developer's machine; no lockfiles; no documented setup | Containerized dev environment; setup documented; builds reproducible most of the time | Hermetic build; dev container or equivalent; automated setup under 10 minutes; agent sandbox with egress policy; pinned dependencies | Automated-setup success rate; reproducible-build rate; median setup time; lockfile presence; pinned-dependency score [114] |
| **R2 Verification signal quality** | No automated tests, or tests routinely skipped | Unit tests on core paths; linting in CI; coverage measured | Tests cover changed lines; flaky tests quarantined below an agreed rate; type checks, static analysis and security scanning in CI with machine-readable results; mutation or contract tests where risk warrants | Coverage on changed lines; flaky-test rate; CI feedback latency; static-analysis findings fed back to the agent; share of merges with green verification |
| **R3 Task specification hygiene** | One-line tickets | Issue templates; acceptance criteria sometimes | Issues carry problem statement, acceptance criteria, scope and non-goals; specs for non-trivial work; acceptance tests written before delegation | Share of agent tasks with acceptance tests; wrong-solution share in failure analysis |
| **R4 Structure and navigability** | Large tangled modules; inconsistent naming; no index | Modular boundaries; consistent naming; code search available | Modules with explicit interfaces; expressive naming; low duplication; code index or graph available to harnesses; code-health score above threshold | Module coupling and file size distributions; duplication rate; code-health score [90]; index freshness; cross-file failure share |
| **R5 Documentation and decisions** | README absent or stale | README with purpose and status; some architecture notes | Current README and architecture docs with verified dates; ADRs for significant decisions; API docs generated from source | Doc currency (days since verified); ADR coverage of major changes; broken-link and stale-reference counts |
| **R6 Context files and procedural knowledge** | None, or generated boilerplate | Context file present, unmaintained | Short, prescriptive, non-derivable context file owned by the team; procedures delivered as on-demand skills or playbooks; staleness reviewed each release | Context-file size, age and owner; instruction-follow rate; incremental token cost per task; skill usage counts |
| **R7 Ownership, workflow and guardrails** | Agent output pasted manually; no owners; direct pushes | CODEOWNERS; PR review; branch protection on main | Owners for all paths; rulesets with required reviews, status checks and signed commits; separate approver for agent PRs; scoped agent identity and credentials; hooks that block prohibited actions; audit trail of agent actions; secrets scanning | Share of agent changes through the governed flow; bypass and exception counts; ownerless-path share; permission-denied and blocked-action counts |
| **R8 Measurement and evaluation** | No measurement | Usage counts | Private eval suite of 20-50 real tasks per task class with pass^k tracked; cost per merged change; review load, rework, defects and vulnerabilities attributed to agent-assisted changes; regression evals on model or harness change | pass^k by task class; cost per merged change; review time per PR; defect and vulnerability density in agent-assisted changes |

Three rules govern scoring.

1. **Minimum across dimensions.** A repository's readiness score is the minimum of its eight dimension scores, with the mean reported alongside for planning. This is deliberately harsh, for the reason the ML Test Score gives: "a system must consider all in order to raise the score" [50]. A repository with excellent context files and no reproducible build produces confident, unverifiable output, which is the failure mode the evidence documents most clearly.
2. **Task-class relativity.** Scores are recorded per task class (for example dependency upgrades, test generation, bug fixes with a reproduction, feature work behind an interface, migrations). A repository can be tier 3 for upgrades and tier 1 for feature work. GitHub's own guidance draws the same line: start with bug fixes, test coverage, documentation and technical-debt reduction; avoid cross-repository refactors and security-sensitive work [71].
3. **Evidence, not assertion.** A dimension cannot score above 2 without telemetry evidence, or 4 without an independent check of that evidence (internal audit, platform team, or a second assessor).

### 3.3 Readiness tiers and what they permit

The minimum score maps to four tiers, which double as governance classifications aligned with the human-in-, over- and out-of-the-loop taxonomy of Singapore's governance frameworks [115, 116].

| Tier | Minimum score | What is permitted | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|---|
| T0 Not ready | 0-1 | Completion assistants only; no autonomous changes | None beyond inventory entry |
| T1 Assisted | 2 | Agents propose changes; humans verify everything; agent PRs cannot be merged without owner review | R1, R2, R7 at 2 or above |
| T2 Delegable with checkpoints | 3 | Bounded task classes delegated with defined checkpoints for irreversible actions; pass^k above an agreed threshold on the private suite for that class | All dimensions at 3 or above for the class; pass^k evidence |
| T3 Autonomous with oversight | 4 | Long-running or scheduled agent work with monitoring, kill switches and periodic audit | All dimensions at 4; monitoring and audit evidence |

The threshold for pass^k is a policy choice, but the benchmark literature suggests the order of magnitude: τ-bench reports pass^8 below 25% in its retail domain for a model whose single-shot success was already under 50%, which is why reliability rather than peak performance must govern delegation [113].

### 3.4 From ready repositories to a mature estate

Readiness is a state of one repository. Maturity is the property of the process that produces and maintains that state across the estate. Following Amershi et al.'s six attributes for any engineering activity (goals defined, consistent, documented, automated, measured, continuously improved) [51] and CMM's staged levels [11], we define five estate-maturity levels.

**Table 3.2. Estate maturity ladder**

| Level | Name | What is true of the estate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ad hoc | Individual teams use agents on whatever repositories they own; no inventory; readiness unknown |
| 2 | Inventoried | Every repository is in an inventory with owner, sensitivity tier, harnesses in use and task classes attempted; context files catalogued |
| 3 | Scored | RARM indicators computed automatically for the repositories carrying most agent traffic; tiers assigned; remediation backlog exists; private eval suites for at least the top task classes |
| 4 | Managed | Indicators computed for the whole estate on a schedule; tier changes require evidence; guardrails enforced by platform (rulesets, hooks, identity); effectiveness measured with DORA, quality and cost outcomes per team; regression evals on model or harness change |
| 5 | Optimizing | Remediation prioritized by measured value; readiness telemetry feeds curation of context files and knowledge sources; agents used to raise readiness (setup automation, test generation, documentation currency) under the same gates; external assurance |

The ladder makes the relationship between the two words precise. An organization can have a handful of ready repositories at level 1 and no ready repositories at level 3; what changes with level is the repeatability and evidential quality of the process, not the state of any single codebase. Level 4 is where regulators' expectations of inventory, materiality, lifecycle controls and monitoring are met for coding agents (Section 5).

### 3.5 How much readiness is enough

The evidence gives a rough production function. Executability and verification (R1, R2) are necessary for any autonomous change and account for most of the difference between benchmark configurations [66, 70, 74]. Structure and specification (R3, R4) determine the ceiling on task complexity [86, 104]. Documentation and context files (R5, R6) shift cost and convention adherence more than success rates [94, 97]. Ownership, workflow and guardrails (R7) determine whether the organization is allowed to use what works [107, 116]. Measurement (R8) determines whether anyone can tell. A regular company with a mixed estate should therefore expect its first year of readiness work to be dominated by builds, tests and tickets rather than by prompts, a conclusion Google's migration report reaches from the other direction: with the estate in place, the bottleneck became "the speed at which engineers could review the changes" [117].

### 3.6 Repository anti-patterns

- **Overview-style context files.** Long, generated repository summaries raise cost and do not raise success [97].
- **Green CI on weak tests.** Passing tests that do not exercise the change give agents and reviewers false confidence [75].
- **Ownerless paths and bypassable rules.** Any path without an owner or any ruleset with a broad bypass list is where agent changes will land unreviewed [108, 109].
- **Setup by folklore.** Undocumented system dependencies and unpinned versions are the leading cause of environment failure [66, 67].
- **Delegating on public benchmarks.** Vendor scores on contaminated public suites are not evidence about a private codebase [70, 75].
- **Measuring exposure.** Share of AI-generated code is an input metric; it predicts neither quality nor value [32, 41].

## 4. What the industry has: landscape, academic guidance and deep case studies

### 4.1 The landscape in one table

Table 4.1 places the instruments an organization is likely to encounter against the three levels of Section 1.2. The pattern is consistent: the organizational level is crowded with descriptive models, the repository level has vendor checklists and benchmarks but no scored model, and the estate level between them is empty.

**Table 4.1. Instruments by level**

| Level | Instrument | What it scores | Validation | Gap for coding agents |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### 4.2 Academic guidance

Four strands of academic work bear directly on the design of a repository readiness model.

**How to build a defensible model.** de Bruin et al.'s phases (scope, design, populate, test, deploy, maintain) [130], Becker et al.'s eight requirements [43] and Pöppelbuß and Röglinger's principles for descriptive and prescriptive use [44] are the checklist RARM was written against; Mettler's warning about bureaucracy without theory is the standing caveat [131]. The AI-specific literature adds that readiness is purpose-specific (Jöhnk et al.'s five categories and 58 indicators) [9], that capability is the ability "to select, orchestrate, and leverage" resources rather than their possession [132], that lower technology-readiness levels demand greater organizational readiness [133], and that readiness is "a degree," not a binary [134]. Sadiq et al.'s finding that fewer than half of AI maturity models are validated is the number that should discipline any new proposal, including this one [45].

**What makes code changeable.** The empirical software-engineering literature predates agents but predicts their behaviour. Ownership concentration predicts defects [107]; low code health multiplies defects fifteenfold and time-to-change by 2.24 [90]; flaky tests are pervasive and have identifiable root causes [76, 77]; documentation is chronically incomplete on purpose and status [95]; and the monorepo trade-offs are visibility and atomic refactoring against tooling investment [92, 93]. Google's engineering-productivity research adds a scoring template, the six attributes of a mature activity [51], and its static-analysis lessons show that findings surfaced at review time are acted on while findings elsewhere are ignored [79].

**What agents need.** Context engineering is now a research field (a 2025 survey covers 1,411 papers) [135]; long-context degradation is replicated across model families [102, 103]; documentation retrieval helps [94]; repository graphs help [88]; instruction files help only when short and prescriptive [97]; and the agentic software engineering roadmap defines briefing, mentoring and merge-readiness as first-class engineering activities [136, 137]. The vibe-coding ethnography finds that expertise is "redistributed toward context management" rather than eliminated [138].

**How to measure effect.** Randomized field experiments rank highest [4, 5, 30], with heterogeneity by experience, task and codebase as the consistent finding; large-N vendor telemetry is directionally useful and observational [34-36]; surveys measure trust and sentiment [8, 42]; and METR's task-substitution note bounds what any task-level speedup can say about value [32].

### 4.3 Deep case study: Google

Google is the clearest public example of an engineering estate that was AI-ready before AI-assisted engineering existed, and of an organization that measures the effect with engineering-productivity research rather than usage counts. Every fact below is from a Google publication or a peer-reviewed paper by Google authors.

**The estate.** Google keeps most of its code in a single repository served by Piper, "a common source of truth for tens of thousands of developers" holding "billions of lines of code" [92]; the monorepo's advantages (visibility, atomic changes, dependency management) and costs (tooling investment) are documented in a 2018 case study [93]. "Essentially every change is reviewed before being committed," readability approvals are granted only to engineers certified in a language, and "only one LGTM is required by default" [78]; the review system was studied across 9 million reviewed changes [139]. Static analysis is integrated into that review flow: Tricorder runs more than 100 analyzers, reviewers click "Please Fix" thousands of times a day, authors apply automated fixes about 3,000 times a day, and the effective false-positive rate is just under 5% [78, 79, 140]. Continuous integration (TAP) handles more than 50,000 unique changes and 4 billion test cases a day, with presubmit reserved for "fast, reliable" tests and hermetic test environments with "no external dependencies like production backends" [78, 141]. Flakiness is managed as an engineering program: about 1.5% of runs flaky, 16% of tests with some flakiness, automatic quarantine and a dedicated team [76]. Coverage is computed for "one billion lines of code daily" and shown "at the level of changesets and code review" [142]; mutation testing reaches 6,000 engineers and about 30% of diffs through the same review surface [143]. Large-scale changes are industrialized: Rosie "shards" a change "based upon project boundaries and ownership rules," routes shards to owners, auto-approves pattern-matching shards, and rode the TAP train to more than 700 independent changes a day at peak, including a migration of 500,000 references and a three-day series that removed more than a billion lines [78].

**The AI practice.** With that estate in place, AI features attached to existing surfaces rather than replacing them. By May 2023, ML-suggested edits addressed 52% of code-review comments, with 40-50% of previewed suggestions applied [144]. By June 2024, AI completions had a 37% acceptance rate and accounted for 50% of code characters, more than 8% of review comments were addressed with AI, and the team's method was explicit: "quick iterations with online A/B experiments are key, as offline metrics are often only rough proxies of user value" [145]. By January 2025, LLM-driven migrations reported that 80% of code modifications in an int32-to-int64 migration were "fully AI-authored," that about 87% of AI-generated JUnit migration code was committed unchanged across 5,359 files, and that "the bottleneck in the process was the speed at which engineers could review the changes" [117]. Google's disclosed share of new code that is AI-generated and approved rose from more than 25% (October 2024) to well over 30% (April 2025) to 75% (April 2026), and an agent-assisted migration completed six times faster than engineers alone a year earlier [6, 41].

**Measurement.** Google's public metric is not the share of AI-generated code but a "carefully measured" increase in engineering velocity of about 10%, defined as hours per week of capacity created [41]; its enterprise RCT with 96 engineers found roughly 21% less time on task with a wide interval [30]; and its migration program defined success as "at least 50% acceleration in task completion rate" end to end, including review and rollout [117]. Google also sponsors the DORA research that supplies the industry's team-level capabilities model [8].

### 4.4 Deep case study: JPMorgan Chase

JPMorgan Chase is the largest technology organization in banking and the most disclosed. It illustrates how a regulated firm moves from assistants to agents while measuring outcomes, and where the readiness gaps remain.

**The estate.** The bank invests "over $18 billion annually in technology," moves "$12 trillion in payments daily" and describes itself as "fueling AI with an exabyte of data" [146]. Its 2025 investor materials reported a $18 billion budget (about 10% of revenue), 65% of workloads on public or private cloud (50% a year earlier; 80% including virtualized), code deployments up 70% over two years and replanned work down 20% [147, 148]; the 2026 budget is about $20 billion [53]. Its technology workforce was reported at 63,000 [149]. Its public GitHub organization holds 74 repositories, including the Salt design system and a widely used Python training course [150].

**The AI program.** LLM Suite, a model-agnostic platform on external models, reached more than 200,000 employees by May 2025 and nearly 250,000 by October, without a usage mandate: "we didn't mandate the use of LLM Suite" [148, 151]. The firm tracked value in two ways, day-to-day productivity and domain-level value in credit, fraud, marketing, technology and operations, with gross AI benefits growing 30-40% a year; consumer banking reported a 35% increase in AI/ML value and the corporate and investment bank more than 175 use cases in production [147, 151]. Roughly 450 AI use cases were targeted to reach 1,000 by 2026 [149].

**The coding program.** A coding assistant deployed to tens of thousands of engineers produced a reported 10-20% efficiency gain in 2025 and a reported 10% in year-end disclosures, methodology undisclosed [149, 152]; the CFO described "vibe coding" among professional engineers as "actually like pretty amazing" [148]. In March 2026, according to internal documents reported by Business Insider, engineering performance expectations were updated to require "measurable improvement in code quality, speed and productivity through regular use of approved AI coding assist tools," with a dashboard classifying engineers as light, heavy or non-users, and a Claude Code pilot was expected in April [153]. In June 2026 the firm said 2026 would bring long-running autonomous agents capable of code generation, browser and desktop interaction, and that "2025 was about ensuring AI agents work effectively, 2026 is about providing appropriate context," with success depending on "strong context, economic guardrails and disciplined governance" [53, 154].

**Governance.** The chief analytics officer flagged agent credential handling as "a real problem" in October 2025 and said the security and governance barriers would be resolved in 2026 [53, 151]; the CEO's 2026 letter committed to redeployment plans for affected staff [155]. As a US bank holding company the firm's model risk framework now sits under SR 26-2, which excludes generative and agentic AI, so its coding-agent controls must be evidenced through enterprise risk, third-party and cyber frameworks rather than model validation [58]. It ranked first in the Evident AI Index of the 50 largest banks in October 2025 [156].

The case is strong on program design and weak on repository-level evidence, which is precisely the layer the paper argues is missing from public discourse.

### 4.5 Comparators

**Goldman Sachs.** The firm rolled its AI assistant out firmwide in June 2025 [158], piloted Cognition's Devin from July 2025 with "hundreds" of agents alongside about 12,000 developers under a "hybrid workforce" model in which engineers "describe problems, turn it into prompts" and "supervise the work of those agents" [159], extended Claude-based agents to reconciliation and onboarding in 2026 [160], and, in an August 2026 profile, framed the metric that matters as idea-to-production speed while noting that agents struggle "when objectives are ambiguous or lack specificity" [161]. Its Legend platform, open-sourced through FINOS in 2020, shows the estate-level investment in shared data and SDLC tooling that precedes agent readiness [162]. Its CIO's argument that AI code "can be syntactically and semantically correct but not functionally correct" is the specification and verification thesis of Section 3 in one sentence [163].

**DBS Bank.** DBS industrialized AI over a decade with an ALAN knowledge repository, an ADA data platform, the PURE responsible-data test and data-chapter members in every squad [164-166], reported AI economic value rising to SGD 1 billion in 2025 across 430 use cases, and an agentic coding tool saving data professionals about 20% of coding time [167]. Its Digital Value Capture accounting, run with McKinsey since 2016, is the model for the value dimension of any maturity assessment [167].

**Citi.** Citi's Devin rollout to about 40,000 developers specified the guardrails before scale: humans keep approval, agents cannot deploy code, access is limited to internal repositories through retrieval, and automated tests run before human review [168]; its disclosed metrics remain activity counts (7 million tool uses, more than a million automated code reviews) [169].

**Morgan Stanley.** The firm's evals-first rollout (advisor-graded summarization and retrieval evals, a daily regression suite) is the readiness gate of R8 applied to a knowledge assistant [170], and its DevGen.AI translated nine million lines of legacy code into specifications that humans reimplemented, keeping people at the point where correctness cannot yet be verified automatically [171].

### 4.6 Cross-case synthesis

Three conclusions hold across the cases. The organizations with the strongest disclosed outcomes (Google, DBS) built the estate first: verification surfaces, ownership routing, knowledge platforms and value accounting. The banks converge on the same guardrail set for agent-authored code (approved tools, internal-only retrieval, tests before review, humans approve, agents cannot deploy) even though no regulator has yet written it down for coding agents. The adoption-policy cases outside banking (Shopify's reflexive-use baseline, Coinbase's onboarding mandate, Salesforce's hiring freeze, Klarna's cost-driven reversal) show what happens when targets precede measurement [39, 172-174]. And every organization's public productivity number is an exposure or efficiency figure with an undisclosed method, while the metric each says it manages on is a capacity or idea-to-production measure. The instrument the industry lacks is the one that would let a bank say, per repository, what an agent may do and show the evidence.

Second, the controls that agents make newly load-bearing are segregation of duties (an agent must never be its own approver, and the person who assigned the task should not be either), provenance (which agent, model, harness and context produced a change) and identity (an agent with a human's credentials is an unmonitored delegation chain). GitHub's coding agent implements the first by design [84]; the second and third are what NIST's agent-identity work and FINOS's framework are formalizing [56, 188].

### 5.2 Design principles for a regulated estate

1. **Agent-authored code is code.** It passes the same gates as human-authored code, plus provenance, segregation of duties and identity controls. No separate, weaker path.
2. **Readiness before autonomy.** Tier assignment (Section 3.3) is the authorization decision. An agent may do on a repository only what that repository's tier and task class permit, and the tier is evidenced, not asserted.
3. **Evidence in the supervisor's shape.** Every readiness indicator and every effectiveness metric is stored so that it can be produced as inventory, materiality, testing, change or monitoring evidence without a separate exercise (Table 5.3).
4. **Minimum across dimensions.** A high average with one zero is a zero, because supervisors and incidents find the weakest control.
5. **Outcome metrics over usage metrics.** Usage supports adoption tracking; delivery, quality, cost and risk outcomes support claims about value; experiments support headcount and investment decisions [123-125].
6. **Data classification governs scope.** Repositories carry a sensitivity tier (Coinbase's "repository sensitivity matrix" is the public example [39]); the tier limits which harnesses, models and retrieval sources may touch the code, and whether production data may appear in any environment an agent reaches.

### 5.3 Repository controls for agent-authored code

The following is the minimum control set that a regulated firm should be able to demonstrate for any repository at tier T1 or above, expressed as what an auditor can check.

- **Identity.** Each agent runs under its own identity with credentials scoped to the repository and task; human credentials are never delegated; credential exposure is monitored [56, 188].
- **Approval.** Rulesets require review from a code owner who is neither the agent nor the assigner; status checks (build, tests on changed lines, static analysis, security scanning, dependency risk, licence scan) are required; bypass lists are empty or audited [108, 109, 111].
- **Provenance.** Commits and PRs carry the agent, model, harness version, context files and skills in effect; where the estate uses build attestations, the agent is recorded as a builder input [182].
- **Environment.** Agents execute in sandboxes with no production data, controlled egress and pinned dependencies; setup is automated and its success rate measured [61, 72, 193].
- **Guardrails.** Harness hooks block prohibited actions (destructive commands, secret access, network egress outside the allowlist) deterministically; MCP servers are allowlisted and version-pinned; tool descriptions and context files are reviewed as supply-chain artifacts [54, 55, 110].
- **Verification.** Automated fixes from AI (for example, security autofixes) are reviewed by a human before acceptance, as the vendor documentation itself requires [194]; flaky tests are quarantined so that green means green.
- **Records.** The PR record states the task, acceptance criteria, verification evidence and rollback path; the audit log of agent actions is retained for the change-record retention period [61, 180].
- **Kill switch and checkpoints.** Long-running or scheduled agents have a documented stop mechanism and human checkpoints before irreversible actions (deployments, schema changes, permission changes) [59, 116].

### 5.6 A 12-month roadmap

**Days 0-90: inventory and baseline (estate level 2 to 3).** Put every harness, model, MCP server and agent in the AI inventory and every repository in a register with owner and sensitivity tier. Publish an AI stance and acceptable-use policy for engineering (DORA capability 1) [8]. Stand up an LLM gateway and a sandboxed execution path with egress policy. Compute RARM indicators automatically for the 20-50 repositories carrying the most agent traffic. Fix the minimum dimension in each; expect it to be builds, flaky tests and ticket hygiene rather than prompts [66, 74, 76]. Replace generated repository overviews with short, team-owned context files [14, 97]. Start private eval suites with 20-50 real tasks for two task classes. Baseline DORA metrics per team.

**Days 90-180: delegation with checkpoints (estate level 3).** Move all agent changes into the governed PR flow with rulesets, CODEOWNERS, separate approvers, scoped agent identities and audit logs [84, 108, 109]. Expose certified knowledge sources (build and test procedures, conventions, runbooks, decision records) through a harness-independent transport with permissions [126, 201]. Promote task classes to T2 only when pass^k clears the threshold. Begin monitoring review load, duplication and vulnerability density on agent-assisted changes [34, 35, 37]. Launch role-based fluency training that includes supervising agents and writing acceptance criteria [159, 202].

**Days 180-365: scale on evidence (estate level 4).** Extend indicator computation to the whole estate. Bring T2 and T3 repositories under independent validation of scores and eval suites, lifetime logging and kill-switch controls; produce the evidence pack (Table 5.3) and map it to the applicable regimes [59, 61, 122]. Run one comparison design on a decision that matters. Re-base efficiency targets only on measured outcomes; publish redeployment plans before headcount decisions [155, 203]. Institute regression evals on every model and harness change and keep the harness swappable [3]. Use agents, under the same gates, to raise readiness: automated setup scripts, test generation for uncovered paths, documentation currency checks (estate level 5).

### 5.7 Anti-patterns specific to regulated firms

- **A separate, lighter path for agent code.** Auditors will find it; so will incidents. Agent-authored code goes through the same gates plus provenance.
- **Agents on human credentials.** An unmonitored delegation chain that no framework permits [56, 188].
- **Usage-tied performance reviews.** They optimize the input metric and invite gaming, and the public examples are in the same sector [125, 153, 157].
- **Efficiency claims with undisclosed methods.** Supervisors examining "AI washing" will ask for the definition [204].
- **Production data in agent sandboxes.** Explicitly prohibited under DORA's technical standards [61].
- **Public benchmarks as delegation evidence.** Contaminated and infrastructure-sensitive [70, 75].
- **Assuming model risk management covers coding agents.** In the US it currently does not; say which framework does [58].

## 6. Limitations and threats to validity

**Evidence base.** The environment-setup and benchmark evidence in Section 3 is recent, mostly on open-source Python and JVM projects, and measured with early-2025 models; enterprise monorepos, proprietary build systems and non-Python stacks are under-studied, and success rates will rise as tooling improves. The productivity evidence remains thin and heterogeneous, and the most rigorous field experiment is one whose authors judge their follow-up design compromised [5, 28]. Survey and vendor-telemetry sources are used for direction, not magnitude.

**Case studies.** Both deep cases rest on company publications, peer-reviewed papers by company authors, investor materials and press reporting. Google's figures are self-reported and its "AI-generated" share is character-level; JPMorgan's efficiency figures are undefined and its 2026 statements are intentions. Where a primary outlet was inaccessible the syndicated version is cited, and secondary reporting of internal documents is labelled as such. Neither firm publishes repository-level readiness data, which is the layer this paper argues is missing; the mappings in Tables 4.2 and 4.3 are therefore readings of the public record, not assessments.

**Regulatory mapping.** Several official texts (MAS Technology Risk Management Guidelines, FFIEC booklet, PCI DSS) were verified through summaries because the issuers' sites refused automated access; article and requirement numbers are as reported in those summaries. Two anticipated instruments were not final at the time of writing: MAS's AI risk management guidelines and the US interagency request for information on generative and agentic AI in model risk management. The EU AI Act analysis assumes coding agents are minimal-risk uses; an organization's specific deployment could change that classification [205].

**Framework validation.** RARM and the estate ladder satisfy the design requirements of comparison with existing models, problem definition, documented construction and prescriptive improvement measures, but they have not been validated against outcomes, which is the deficiency Sadiq et al. found in most AI maturity models [43, 45]. Indicator thresholds are provisional; the minimum-across rule and the pass^k gate are borrowed from adjacent domains. Validation would require a panel of organizations scoring repositories, assigning tiers and tracking delivery, quality and value outcomes over at least a year, ideally with staggered adoption, and the author would welcome collaborators.

**Scope.** The paper covers code repositories and coding agents. Enterprise agents in operations and customer service share the estate-maturity ladder and the governance controls but need different readiness indicators. The author works inside a large regulated organization and weights governance evidence accordingly; readers elsewhere may reasonably weight it lower.

## 7. Conclusion

The question this paper set out to answer, how AI maturity can be assessed so that it measures effectiveness and readiness, has an answer that the industry has been circling for three years without naming. The unit of readiness for coding agents is the repository, and the unit of maturity is the process that keeps repositories ready. Every strand of evidence points there: the benchmarks that double when tasks and environments are fixed, the flaky tests that make green meaningless, the code-health economics that multiply defects and change time, the instruction files that help only when they are short and prescriptive, the ownership rules that predict where defects and unreviewed changes land, and the two deep cases, in which the organization that built its estate first reports the strongest outcomes and the regulated bank diagnoses its own 2026 constraint as context and guardrails rather than models.

RARM makes that answer operational: eight dimensions with computable indicators, scored on the minimum, per task class, mapped to tiers that double as authorizations, under an estate ladder that turns readiness into a repeatable and auditable process. For a regulated firm, the good news is that the controls an agent-authored change must satisfy already exist in its SDLC; what agents add is provenance, segregation of duties and identity, and what the readiness pipeline adds is the evidence. The organizations that will report value from coding agents in 2027 are the ones whose builds are hermetic, whose tests mean something, whose tickets say what done looks like, whose owners are named, and whose measurements survive an auditor. None of that requires a frontier model. All of it requires deciding that the repository is where AI maturity is assessed.
